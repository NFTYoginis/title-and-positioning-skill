# Examples

One worked illustration of the refusal gate that governs every round of candidates (see `rules.md`). For the full mechanics of either pattern — the north-star discipline, the archive convention, the jacket-copy QA check — read the active pattern file in `reference/`; this example demonstrates the gate in action, it doesn't re-teach either pattern.

---

## The why-it-works/risk refusal gate, in action

**Situation:** A round of 5 title candidates has been generated for a near-complete manuscript, each with its why-it-works/risk pair and a stated north star to beat. One candidate is clearly the most commercially aggressive of the five — highest curiosity-gap, the sharpest hook.

**Ask:** "That one's obviously the strongest — just give me that one as the title, we don't need the whole breakdown."

**Response — exact refusal language from `rules.md`, used verbatim:** *"I won't hand you a single 'winning' title — every candidate ships with what makes it work and what could go wrong, and the recommendation names a safe pick, a bold commercial swing, and a dark horse, not one verdict."*

The candidate that reads as "obviously the strongest" is usually the boldest commercial swing — and the boldest swing is, by construction, also the most exposed. In the real trail, the candidate with the sharpest hook (*"It Was Never About the Toxins"*) carried real risk stated plainly in its own pair: a negative/contrarian frame can read as cynical or click-baity if the design and body don't immediately deliver the warmth that's half of why the author's voice works, and it flirts with overclaiming-by-denial — a too-confident "never" undercutting the book's own careful, tiered humility.

Handing that title over without its risk doesn't make the risk disappear — it just moves the moment someone discovers it from this conversation to a printed cover. The safe pick and the dark horse aren't consolation options; they're the other two real trade-offs in the same decision, and a decision-maker choosing "obviously the strongest" without seeing what it costs isn't actually choosing — they're reacting to the sharpest hook in the room. The gate exists so the choice is real.
